Kerberos Decoder — read a v5 message, ticket or keytab

Paste the bytes of a Kerberos v5 message — out of a Wireshark capture, out of a log, out of a debugger — and see every field of it, named, with the encryption types and flags spelled out rather than left as numbers. AS-REQ, AS-REP, TGS-REQ, TGS-REP, AP-REQ, AP-REP, KRB-ERROR, a bare Ticket, an Authenticator, and the decrypted Enc*Part structures. Anything else is shown as its ASN.1 structure, which is more useful than a refusal.

Input may be base64, base64url or hex, with or without spaces, colons or 0x prefixes — so a Wireshark "copy as hex stream" and a C array both work as pasted. Kerberos over TCP puts a four-byte length in front of every message; if that prefix is present it is detected, removed, and said so. Leaving it in place is what makes an ASN.1 parser fail on byte zero and blame ASN.1.

This page does no networking. It talks to no KDC and needs no back end, which is why it works on the deployed site while the rest of the Kerberos workflow — which needs a TCP socket to port 88 — does not. It also stores nothing: not the message, not a password, not a keytab. Everything you give it is key material or a credential, and a scratchpad that remembered those would be a worse place to leave a keytab than the file it came from.

The bytes
Keys, to see inside the encrypted parts (optional)

Most of what is interesting in a Kerberos message is encrypted, and under three different keys: a ticket is sealed with the service's long-term key, an AS-REP's enc-part with the client's, and an Authenticator with the session key from inside the ticket. Supply whichever you have; each part reports which key opened it, or says what would have.

The salt is not something to guess. Take it from the KDC's own PA-ETYPE-INFO2, which arrives inside the KDC_ERR_PREAUTH_REQUIRED error — paste that error in here and this page will read the salt out of it for you. Active Directory's default is the realm followed by the sAMAccountName for a user, but a host-shaped string for a computer account, which is where guessing stops working.

A keytab holds a service's long-term keys, so it is what opens a service ticket. Both MIT formats are read (0x0501 little-endian and 0x0502 big-endian), and deleted slots are skipped rather than treated as the end of the file.

What a decoder cannot tell you. That a message is well formed and decrypts is not the same as it being accepted: a KDC also weighs its own clock, its own policy, and the account's state. Where this page can measure such a thing it does — a KRB-ERROR carries the KDC's own timestamp, so the clock difference against this browser is computed rather than guessed — and where it cannot, it says so instead of implying a verdict.
