Decryption keys — open the encrypted parts

Most of what matters in these messages is encrypted, and under three different keys: a ticket is sealed with the service principal's own long-term key (for a TGT that is the KDC's krbtgt key), an AS-REP's enc-part with the client's, and an Authenticator with the session key from inside the ticket. This page already holds the ones it earned — the key it derived from your password, the session keys of the tickets it obtained — so what belongs here is the one a client never has: the key of the principal named in the ticket's sname. Supply whichever you have and press the button; every encrypted part then reports which key opened it, or what was tried and failed.

A raw key as hex — from a keytab read elsewhere, or derived by hand. This is what opens a ticket: the long-term key of the principal in its sname.
Which encryption type the raw key above belongs to. A key of the wrong etype is not tried at all, and one of the right etype that is the wrong key fails integrity — which looks identical unless the pane says which happened.
The password of the account whose key you need — the service account, or krbtgt for a ticket-granting ticket. Never sent anywhere: it is turned into a key here, in the browser.
The string mixed into string-to-key. Active Directory uses the realm plus the sAMAccountName for a user but a host-shaped string for a computer account, so it cannot be computed from the principal name — the KDC publishes it in PA-ETYPE-INFO2 and nowhere else.
A keytab, base64 or hex. Every usable key in it is offered, which is the quickest route when you do not know which etype the ticket used.
Which key opens what, and why this is not a way in

A ticket carries no hint of its own key beyond the etype and a kvno, so a key of the right type that does not work is either a different principal's or a stale version — and the pane says which of the two states it is in rather than reporting both as a failure. Key usage numbers are not interchangeable either: a ticket is at 2, an AS-REP's enc-part at 3, an Authenticator at 11, a TGS-REP's enc-part at 8 or 9 depending on whether a subkey was sent. Trying every usage until one worked would sometimes succeed with the wrong number and present a confident wrong answer, so each part is tried only at the usage it is defined to use.

Nothing here weakens anything: supplying a key you already hold is how a service reads a ticket presented to it, and that is the position this pane puts you in. What it cannot do is open a ticket whose key you do not have — there is no attack in here, and a wrong key produces an integrity failure rather than plausible plaintext.

Nothing typed here is stored, in local or session storage, and nothing is sent to the api: the decryption happens in this browser. The keys therefore last as long as this page is open, which is the deliberate cost of not keeping a long-term key in a debugger.
