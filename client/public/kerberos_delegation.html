Kerberos — delegation: acting as somebody else

Four things a ticket-granting ticket can be spent on that are not "get me a service ticket", and they are on one page because the interesting part is the comparison. A reader who has seen only one of them has the wrong model of the others. Get a TGT first if you have none.

S4U2Self is not a privilege and S4U2Proxy is. The first gets a service a ticket to itself, naming a user who takes no part in it — no password, no ticket of theirs, nothing they consented to. Anyone with a service account can do that, because the ticket is to themselves. The second reaches another service as that user, and what stands between the two is one attribute on one account. Two different attributes can grant it, on opposite accounts: msDS-AllowedToDelegateTo on the front end (classic constrained delegation, which only a domain admin can set) or msDS-AllowedToActOnBehalfOfOtherIdentity on the back end (resource-based, which whoever controls that object can set). That inversion is why resource-based delegation turns “I can write to this computer account” into “I can reach this service as anybody”.

The ticket-granting ticket you hold

Whose TGT this should be depends on which half you are doing. S4U2Self and S4U2Proxy are requests a service makes, so for those the ticket wanted here is the service account's own — authenticate on the AS page as HTTP/frontend.example.com rather than as a user. Forwarding and renewal are things a user's client does with its own.

S4U2Self — a ticket to myself, as somebody else
The user the service will act as. S4U2Self asks the KDC for a ticket to ITSELF naming this user, which is how a service obtains evidence for someone who never authenticated to it.

This account takes no part in the exchange. The request is a TGS-REQ for the service itself, carrying PA-FOR-USER — whose keyed checksum is HMAC-MD5 at key usage 17 over the name type as four little-endian bytes then the name components, the realm and the auth-package, concatenated. That is HMAC-MD5 whatever the session key's own encryption type, which is a detail almost every implementation gets wrong once. The checksum proves only that whoever built the padata holds the TGT's session key: it is integrity, not authorization.

The evidence ticket

Watch whether it came back forwardable. Classic constrained delegation requires forwardable evidence and resource-based does not, and a ticket out of S4U2Self is forwardable only when the requesting account has TRUSTED_TO_AUTHENTICATE_FOR_DELEGATION set. So a missing flag on the front-end account shows up two steps later as S4U2Proxy complaining about the evidence — which is not where the problem is. That is why this pane says so at the moment the ticket arrives.

S4U2Proxy — reach another service as that user
The back-end SPN to reach on the user's behalf. For classic delegation it must appear in the front end's msDS-AllowedToDelegateTo, exactly.
Use the resource-based route, which sends PA-PAC-OPTIONS. The permission then lives on the TARGET as msDS-AllowedToActOnBehalfOfOtherIdentity rather than on the front end — the inversion that is the whole security story of RBCD. Without this option a KDC that would only allow that route answers KDC_ERR_BADOPTION, mentioning nothing about padata.

Tick that box when resource-based delegation is what would permit this. [MS-SFU] requires a KDC to answer KDC_ERR_BADOPTION when RBCD is the only thing authorizing a request and this padata is absent — and that error says nothing whatsoever about padata, so it is a refusal worth being able to produce on purpose. Classic constrained delegation does not need it.

The request sets cname-in-addl-tkt and carries the evidence ticket in additional-tickets. The KDC reads the client's identity out of that ticket rather than out of the TGT — which is what makes the reply a ticket for the user rather than for the service asking.

Forwarding — unconstrained delegation

The oldest form, and by far the most dangerous. The client asks for a second ticket-granting ticket flagged forwarded and hands it to a service inside a KRB-CRED. After that the service can obtain tickets to anything as that client until the ticket expires, and the KDC is never asked again — there is no list of permitted targets, because there is no constraint. Compare the two mechanisms above, where every hop returns to the KDC and is checked against an attribute. A service's ok-as-delegate flag is only advice to the client: nothing enforces it, so the decision is the client's to get right.

The one control that does enforce something sits on the account being protected: NOT_DELEGATED (“account is sensitive and cannot be delegated”). A KDC refuses such an account a forwardable ticket at the AS exchange — not with an error, but by omitting the flag, so the user still logs on and no service anywhere can forward those credentials.

Renewal — the same ticket, later

Not delegation, and here because it is the other thing done with a TGT. A renewable ticket is not an immortal one: the new expiry is capped at renew-till, which does not move, and authtime is preserved. That second rule matters — a renewed ticket must not look freshly authenticated, or a service reading authtime to judge how recently the user proved themselves is told a lie that grows with every renewal. This page asks for longer than renew-till deliberately, so the cap is visible rather than theoretical.

Where the KDC is
The Kerberos realm, conventionally the DNS domain upper-cased. It is case-sensitive on the wire and a realm that differs only in case is a different realm.
Where the KDC is. Resolved and reached by the api's relay, not by the browser — Kerberos is DER over a socket and a page cannot open one.
88 by convention, on both TCP and UDP. The api will only reach ports on its krb5AllowedPorts list, so a non-standard port has to be added there too.
UDP first is the traditional client behaviour, falling back to TCP when the reply will not fit. A reply carrying a PAC usually will not, so most real exchanges end up on TCP.
